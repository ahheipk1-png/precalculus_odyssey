## Prerequisite Review
- An exponent tells how many times a base is used as a factor.
- Exponential and logarithmic forms describe the same relationship.
- The base of a logarithm must be positive and not equal to 1.
- The argument of a logarithm must be positive.
- Common log means base 10, and natural log means base e.

## Core Concepts
- log_b(a) = c means b^c = a.
- A logarithm answers the question: what exponent goes on the base?
- Logarithmic functions are inverses of exponential functions with the same base.
- Logarithms of powers of the base evaluate cleanly.
- The domain restriction comes from the argument being positive.
- Exact log values often come from rewriting numbers as powers.

## Common Misconceptions
- Swapping the base and the argument when converting forms.
- Treating log_b(a) as b times a.
- Allowing zero or negative arguments in real logarithms.
- Forgetting that log(x) means log base 10 in many precalculus contexts.
- Assuming every logarithm has a whole-number value.

# Part III - Tutorial Bible

## Learning Goal
Master logarithm definitions by choosing the right representation, completing the computation, and interpreting the result.

## Why It Matters
This topic appears in later modeling, graphing, and boss challenges because it connects symbolic work with decisions about structure, restrictions, and meaning.

## Prerequisite Check
- Can the player explain this prerequisite? An exponent tells how many times a base is used as a factor.
- Can the player explain this prerequisite? Exponential and logarithmic forms describe the same relationship.
- Can the player explain this prerequisite? The base of a logarithm must be positive and not equal to 1.
- Can the player explain this prerequisite? The argument of a logarithm must be positive.

## Core Concept
- log_b(a) = c means b^c = a.
- A logarithm answers the question: what exponent goes on the base?
- Logarithmic functions are inverses of exponential functions with the same base.
- Logarithms of powers of the base evaluate cleanly.
- The domain restriction comes from the argument being positive.
- Exact log values often come from rewriting numbers as powers.

## Worked Example
- Rewrite log_2(8) = 3 in exponential form. Answer: 2^3 = 8 Reason: The base of the log is 2, the output is 3, and the argument is 8, so 2^3 = 8.
- Rewrite 5^2 = 25 in logarithmic form. Answer: log_5(25) = 2 Reason: The base stays 5, the result 25 becomes the log argument, and the exponent 2 becomes the log value.
- Evaluate log_3(81). Answer: 4 Reason: Since 3^4 = 81, log_3(81) = 4.
- Evaluate log_5(1/25). Answer: -2 Reason: Since 5^-2 = 1/25, the logarithm equals -2.

## Common Mistakes
- Swapping the base and the argument when converting forms.
- Treating log_b(a) as b times a.
- Allowing zero or negative arguments in real logarithms.
- Forgetting that log(x) means log base 10 in many precalculus contexts.
- Assuming every logarithm has a whole-number value.
