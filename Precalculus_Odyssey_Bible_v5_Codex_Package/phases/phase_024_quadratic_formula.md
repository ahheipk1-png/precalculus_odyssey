# Phase 024 - Quadratic formula

## Phase Metadata
- Course: Precalculus Odyssey Bible v5
- Topic: Quadratic formula
- Subtopic: Solving quadratic equations with the quadratic formula and interpreting exact solutions
- Prerequisites: standard form quadratics, square roots, simplifying radicals, discriminant basics
- Related phases: Phase 023 review; Phase 025 follow-up; mixed review and final boss integration
- Estimated phase size: 20 template families, 20 core examples, 120 progressive hints, 1 tutorial sequence, 1 Socratic dialogue system

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this phase, the player can:

1. Recognize the structure of quadratic formula tasks before choosing a method.
2. Use correct notation, restrictions, and units while solving quadratic formula questions.
3. Solve tasks involving identify coefficients.
4. Solve tasks involving simple formula factoring match.
5. Solve tasks involving negative b substitution.
6. Solve tasks involving leading coefficient.
7. Solve tasks involving no integer roots exact.
8. Solve tasks involving simplify radical.
9. Solve tasks involving equation not standard.
10. Solve tasks involving missing b term.

## Prerequisite Review
- A quadratic equation in standard form is ax^2+bx+c=0.
- The quadratic formula is x=(-b+-sqrt(b^2-4ac))/(2a).
- The values a, b, and c come from standard form.
- The expression b^2-4ac is the discriminant.
- Exact radical answers are often preferred over decimals.

## Core Concepts
- Use the quadratic formula when factoring is hard or impossible.
- Move all terms to one side before identifying a, b, and c.
- Substitute coefficients carefully, especially when b or c is negative.
- Simplify the discriminant before simplifying the final radical.
- The denominator is the whole quantity 2a.
- Check solutions by substitution when possible.

## Common Misconceptions
- Using b instead of -b in the numerator.
- Forgetting that b^2 is positive even when b is negative.
- Leaving the equation outside standard form before reading coefficients.
- Dividing only the radical by 2a instead of the entire numerator.
- Rounding too early when exact form is requested.

# Part III - Tutorial Bible

## Learning Goal
Master quadratic formula by choosing the right representation, completing the computation, and interpreting the result.

## Why It Matters
This topic appears in later modeling, graphing, and boss challenges because it connects symbolic work with decisions about structure, restrictions, and meaning.

## Prerequisite Check
- Can the player explain this prerequisite? A quadratic equation in standard form is ax^2+bx+c=0.
- Can the player explain this prerequisite? The quadratic formula is x=(-b+-sqrt(b^2-4ac))/(2a).
- Can the player explain this prerequisite? The values a, b, and c come from standard form.
- Can the player explain this prerequisite? The expression b^2-4ac is the discriminant.

## Core Concept
- Use the quadratic formula when factoring is hard or impossible.
- Move all terms to one side before identifying a, b, and c.
- Substitute coefficients carefully, especially when b or c is negative.
- Simplify the discriminant before simplifying the final radical.
- The denominator is the whole quantity 2a.
- Check solutions by substitution when possible.

## Worked Example
- For 3x^2-5x+2=0, identify a, b, and c. Answer: a=3, b=-5, c=2 Reason: Read directly from ax^2+bx+c=0.
- Solve x^2-5x+6=0 using the quadratic formula. Answer: x=2 or x=3 Reason: Here a=1,b=-5,c=6. The formula gives (5+-sqrt(25-24))/2=(5+-1)/2.
- Solve x^2+4x-5=0 using the quadratic formula. Answer: x=1 or x=-5 Reason: a=1,b=4,c=-5. The formula gives (-4+-sqrt(16+20))/2=(-4+-6)/2.
- Solve 2x^2+3x-2=0. Answer: x=1/2 or x=-2 Reason: a=2,b=3,c=-2. The discriminant is 25, so x=(-3+-5)/4.

## Common Mistakes
- Using b instead of -b in the numerator.
- Forgetting that b^2 is positive even when b is negative.
- Leaving the equation outside standard form before reading coefficients.
- Dividing only the radical by 2a instead of the entire numerator.
- Rounding too early when exact form is requested.
