# Phase 055 - Conic sections

## Phase Metadata
- Course: Precalculus Odyssey Bible v5
- Topic: Conic sections
- Subtopic: Parabolas, circles, ellipses, and hyperbolas in standard form
- Prerequisites: coordinate plane, distance formula, completing the square, quadratic graphs
- Related phases: Phase 054 review; Phase 056 follow-up; mixed review and final boss integration
- Estimated phase size: 20 template families, 20 core examples, 120 progressive hints, 1 tutorial sequence, 1 Socratic dialogue system

## Learning Objectives
By the end of this phase, the player can:

1. Recognize the structure of conic sections tasks before choosing a method.
2. Use correct notation, restrictions, and units while solving conic sections questions.
3. Solve tasks involving classifying a circle from standard form.
4. Solve tasks involving reading circle features.
5. Solve tasks involving writing a circle equation.
6. Solve tasks involving completing the square for a circle.
7. Solve tasks involving finding parabola focus and directrix.
8. Solve tasks involving writing a parabola from vertex and focus.
9. Solve tasks involving reading ellipse vertices.
10. Solve tasks involving reading hyperbola features.

## Prerequisite Review
- Use the distance formula to measure from a point to a center.
- Complete the square by adding the same square-making constant inside the equation.
- Recognize horizontal and vertical translations from expressions like x - h and y - k.
- Connect squared terms to graph shapes on the coordinate plane.
- Check whether a point satisfies an equation by substitution.

## Core Concepts
- A circle has equal squared denominators and stores all points a fixed distance from its center.
- A parabola has one squared variable and is controlled by the directed distance p from vertex to focus.
- An ellipse has two added squared terms with positive denominators and a longer major axis.
- A hyperbola has two squared terms that subtract and has asymptotes through its center.
- Completing the square reveals the translated center or vertex before graph features are read.

## Common Misconceptions
- Treating every equation with squared variables as a circle.
- Forgetting to add square-completion constants to the opposite side of the equation.
- Reading the center signs directly instead of reversing the signs in x - h and y - k.
- Using a^2 - b^2 for hyperbola foci instead of a^2 + b^2.
- Calling the larger denominator a horizontal axis without checking which variable owns it.

# Part III - Tutorial Bible

## Learning Goal
Master conic sections by choosing the right representation, completing the computation, and interpreting the result.

## Why It Matters
This topic appears in later modeling, graphing, and boss challenges because it connects symbolic work with decisions about structure, restrictions, and meaning.

## Prerequisite Check
- Can the player explain this prerequisite? Use the distance formula to measure from a point to a center.
- Can the player explain this prerequisite? Complete the square by adding the same square-making constant inside the equation.
- Can the player explain this prerequisite? Recognize horizontal and vertical translations from expressions like x - h and y - k.
- Can the player explain this prerequisite? Connect squared terms to graph shapes on the coordinate plane.

## Core Concept
- A circle has equal squared denominators and stores all points a fixed distance from its center.
- A parabola has one squared variable and is controlled by the directed distance p from vertex to focus.
- An ellipse has two added squared terms with positive denominators and a longer major axis.
- A hyperbola has two squared terms that subtract and has asymptotes through its center.
- Completing the square reveals the translated center or vertex before graph features are read.

## Worked Example
- Classify the conic x^2 + y^2 = 25 and name its center and radius. Answer: Circle with center (0, 0) and radius 5. Reason: Both squared terms are added with the same coefficient, so the graph is a circle. Since x^2 + y^2 = 5^2, the center is (0, 0) and the radius is 5.
- Find the center and radius of (x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 49. Answer: Center (3, -2), radius 7. Reason: A circle in standard form is (x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2. Here h = 3, k = -2, and r^2 = 49, so r = 7.
- Write the equation of the circle with center (-1, 4) and radius 6. Answer: (x + 1)^2 + (y - 4)^2 = 36. Reason: Use (x - h)^2 + (y - k)^2 = r^2. With h = -1, k = 4, and r = 6, the equation is (x + 1)^2 + (y - 4)^2 = 36.
- Rewrite x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 4y - 12 = 0 in standard form and identify the circle. Answer: (x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 25; center (3, -2), radius 5. Reason: Group terms: x^2 - 6x and y^2 + 4y. Add 9 and 4 to complete squares, giving (x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 = 25. The center is (3, -2), and the radius is 5.

## Common Mistakes
- Treating every equation with squared variables as a circle.
- Forgetting to add square-completion constants to the opposite side of the equation.
- Reading the center signs directly instead of reversing the signs in x - h and y - k.
- Using a^2 - b^2 for hyperbola foci instead of a^2 + b^2.
- Calling the larger denominator a horizontal axis without checking which variable owns it.
